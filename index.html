Tips and Tricks

Practical notes for receiving a steel building kit and putting the frame, bracing, and panels together. Keep the stamped engineered plans in charge. Approved details and local requirements govern the work on your site.

Start with your engineered plans

If the documents disagree, the stamped engineered plans govern. Stop and ask your project team about an unclear detail before you drill, cut, or leave a brace out.

Keep these on site, or where the crew can open them:

  • Stamped engineered plans
  • Construction package
  • Bill of materials
  • Packing slip

The construction package helps with assembly and with identifying material. It can leave out some of the bracing, or all of it. The same wall can show the framing clearly in the construction package and show the required bracing only on the stamped set. Build from the stamped set.

When you ask about a detail, send a clear photo or screenshot of the exact spot on the engineered plans, and a photo of the condition on site. Your project team can match the question to the drawing.

Receive, inventory, and stage the kit

Review the detail drawings before the truck arrives. Note the longest panels and framing members on the bill of materials so the lifting equipment matches the load. Unloading to a plan you made ahead of time saves handling later.

Trucks are loaded for trailer weight, efficiency, and the factory’s safety procedure. The factory does not rearrange a load to a customer’s preferred sequence. Stage the pieces from the load you receive.

As the truck is unloaded

  • Arrange suitable lifting equipment to take the material off the truck.
  • Unload near where the pieces will be used, so the crew is not carrying them across the site.
  • If you unload onto a slab, use ramps so the concrete is not damaged.
  • Put blocking under columns and rafters so splice plates and the slab are protected.
  • Handle panels so the ends, sides, and ribs are not bent.

Inventory immediately

  • Start the count as soon as the material is on site.
  • Cross-check the packing slip, then the bill of materials for quantities and types.
  • Use the construction package and the engineered plans when a piece’s job is unclear.
  • Keep the shipping documents and the sales order confirmation together.
  • Record the order code, description, piece mark, quantity, what is missing, and lengths where they apply.
  • Photograph shortages, damage, and incorrect fabrication. If a piece is the wrong length or punched incorrectly, include a tape measure in the photo.

Report missing or damaged items promptly under your order’s terms. Ask your project manager for the current claim procedure and include the inventory check sheet. Print a blank inventory checklist. A short inventory walk-through is at Inventory instructional video.

Keep steel dry

  • Store steel dry, with air moving through the stack, so storage stain does not form.
  • Set panels on wood blocking 7 to 9 inches high.
  • Cover panels so water does not pond on them. Leave a path for air. An airtight cover that traps moisture works against you.
  • For a long outdoor stack, incline the material so water runs off and the stack can dry. Do not leave puddles on the steel.
  • Dry the material, and store it in a warm, dry place when you can.

If more than one building arrives at once, read the order-number labels and stage each package on its own. Do not mix pieces between orders.

The receiving notes say the factory is not responsible for deterioration or corrosion caused by atmospheric or environmental conditions. That is a statement in those notes about factory responsibility. It is not a summary of your NuEco order. Your order terms control storage claims.

Tools, layout, and framing preparation

Have the work basics on site before you start layout. This is a preparation list from the installer notes. It is not a complete safety plan or an equipment specification.

  • Impact wrench
  • Magnetic nut drivers
  • Chalk line
  • Vise grips
  • Nibbler or shears
  • Caulking gun
  • Ladders
  • Safety gear for the tasks on that site

Mark the slab before you drill

Chalk the bay lines, the column lines, and the anchor-bolt locations before drilling. Check those marks against the approved drawings. A chalk line does not correct a slab that is out of square, and it does not authorize moving an anchor off the drawing. Check the anchor locations again before the bit goes in. A mis-drilled anchor is a costly delay.

Prepare pieces before you raise them

When the erection plan allows it, install clips, cope the braces, and preassemble components on the ground. Plumb the columns early and put in the temporary bracing the erector requires. The installer notes suggest temporary strap X-bracing until the permanent bracing is installed. An unspecified strap layout is not an erection design. A qualified erector sets the sequence, the lifting method, and what keeps the frame stable while it is going up.

When a screw has a sealing washer, compress the washer. Do not crush it. A deformed washer means remove that fastener and install a new screw and washer, following the fastener maker’s instructions.

Screws that attach studs or other connections are specific to that joint. Use the type, length, count, and spacing on the approved detail. A product photo in a tip sheet is not approval to use that screw everywhere.

Crews new to this framing will see girts, purlins, braces, and closures on the drawings. The engineered plans define those parts for your building.

Install the bracing the plans show

Install every brace shown on your building’s stamped engineered plans, with the material, attachment, screw type, and quantity those plans specify. A frame that feels stable is not a substitute for that bracing. When a building fails, bracing is one of the first things inspected. Skipping a brace because the frame feels steady enough leaves the building, the occupants, and the installer exposed.

Buildings differ. Yours may use the systems below, extra bracing, or only some of these. Bold, dashed, and solid lines on an example drawing are examples. They are not a legend you can assume on every engineer’s set. Do not copy fastener counts or member sizes from an example detail onto another building.

X-bracing

Roof and wall bays that need X-bracing are marked on the stamped plans. It is especially common where the walls are not moment frames and the roof is not counting on a sheeting diaphragm. The notes describe flat strap, fastened with screws, rather than the tensioned cables used on some red-iron frames. The plans name the bays and the attachment, including screw type and count.

Fly bracing

Fly bracing is often on endwall columns, main columns, and rafters. It helps resist lateral twisting, sometimes called belly roll. Find the locations on the stamped plans. A matching detail gives the attachment for your building.

Flange bracing

Flange bracing does similar work for girts and purlins, rather than for columns and rafters. On example plans it can appear as dashed lines in the walls and roof. Where a strap would stop at an opening header or sill, the plans may call for an angle brace instead. Use that only when your drawings show it.

Eave-purlin bracing

This brace is commonly an angle rather than a strap. On the roof diagram it is often a solid line from the eave to the nearest purlin. It may need to be coped or bent in the field. The member and the fasteners come from your project detail.

During erection, temporary bracing keeps the unfinished frame stable. Permanent bracing is what the stamped plans require in the finished building. Both belong in the erector’s sequence. One does not replace the other.

Panels, trim, and water

Treat the notes below as planning tips. The approved panel-system instructions and your plans control lap direction, fastener layout, and trim.

  • The installer notes suggest starting the roof sheets on the side opposite the prevailing wind, to reduce water blowing back under the laps. If the panel system specifies a different direction, follow the panel system.
  • A string line at the ridge helps the roof run straight. Align the sheets by the top edge, not the bottom edge.
  • Cut sheets with snips, a nibbler, or shears. Do not cut them with a torch or an abrasive wheel. Those methods damage the coating.
  • Lap and trim so water sheds away from the building.
  • Where gutters, the ridge, or a splice need sealant, use a sealant the panel manufacturer approves as compatible with the finish. Do not assume a general silicone is the right product.

The installation notes point to an external overhang reference with an endwall soffit diagram, a sidewall soffit diagram, and install instructions. Use it only as a reference. Overhang details (external reference). Your stamped plans control the overhang on your building. Those diagrams are not reproduced here, and they are not a complete overhang design.

Openings, notches, and service penetrations

Get project-specific approval before you drill, notch, or cut a structural member. A jamb detail and a primary frame member are different conditions. The approved drawings control both.

Notching around a jamb

Some openings need a specific coping or notching detail. Confirm that detail with your project team before anyone cuts a framing member.

The drawing shows a horizontal member notched so it can fit around a vertical jamb. Clips and framing tek screws connect it at the portal frame and the apex purlin. The drawing does not show the jamb itself being cut. It gives no notch size, steel grade, or fastener schedule, and it is not a certified engineering detail. Do not read it as permission to notch structural steel in general.

PEX and other holes

Planning to route PEX or other services through steel framing? Ask your project engineer to review the exact hole size, quantity, and location before drilling. Limited penetrations may be feasible in some designs, but approval is project-specific.

A conditional opinion that small holes can sometimes be accommodated is not a drilling specification. It does not give a hole diameter, a count, spacing, edge distance, or a load. It also does not evaluate rubber grommets or other tubing protection, so a grommet is not an approved detail on the strength of that opinion.

Equipment for unloading and erection

Choose lifting equipment from the longest panels and framing members on the bill of materials. Use rated equipment, rated rigging, and a qualified operator. The receiving notes mention spreading forks as wide as the load allows, using nylon ropes when a heavy lift carries material, and taking care on uneven ground so panels do not buckle. Those are handling cautions. They are not a rigging plan.

Handle material safely and protect the finish

The installer is responsible for safe working conditions and for preventing accidents. Owners, architects, engineers, contractors, subcontractors, delivery people, and employees all watch for hazards that can damage property or injure someone, including themselves. This page does not replace a site-specific safety plan or the local, state, and federal safety and health rules that apply to the job.

On the jobsite

  • Know the safety and health rules that apply, and see that people on the job follow them.
  • Give clear instructions for installing the building safely.
  • Provide first aid, emergency phone numbers, and emergency procedures.
  • Stop work in weather that puts people or the unfinished structure at risk. Wind can take a panel out of someone’s hands even when the panel is held low.
  • Require the protective gear the task needs. The notes name hard hats, rubber-soled shoes, and safety harnesses, plus whatever else the site requires.
  • The installer secures the site against theft and vandalism while the material is there.

Panels in hand and underfoot

  • Edges and corners of panels and trim are sharp, often before anyone notices. Wear gloves.
  • The notes say to carry panels perpendicular to the ground, with two or more people, so the sheet is not kinked. The crew and the equipment still have to suit the sheet’s size, weight, and the wind.
  • Spread forks wide under a bundle. Take care over uneven ground so the load does not buckle.
  • Dew, frost, and other moisture make panels slippery. Assume the surface is wet.
  • Where the panel manufacturer allows foot traffic, walk the flats, not the ribs, and put your feet over the supports below. Do not assume a panel is walkable. Follow that manufacturer’s limits and use fall protection.
  • Do not walk on panels that are still on the ground.
  • Do not walk on clear or translucent skylight panels.

Cleaning the finish

A factory-applied pre-painted finish can last many years. Dirt, or slight chalking in strong sun, can change the look. A careful wash often restores it, so repainting is not the first step.

  • For routine cleaning, use plain water from a hose or a pressure sprayer.
  • For heavier dirt, use a soft cloth or a soft-bristle brush with a mild detergent the coating manufacturer approves, then rinse thoroughly with clean water.
  • Do not use a wire brush, an abrasive, or a strong solvent. They can scratch or cut the coating.
  • Do not mix cleaners. For mildew, or for caulk, oil, grease, tar, or wax, ask the coating manufacturer which cleaner is approved for that finish. Follow the label for protective gear, ventilation, and disposal, and test the cleaner the way the label directs.
